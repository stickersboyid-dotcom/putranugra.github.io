Solving the right problem to make operations 70% faster

Internal Ops Dashboard · 2024

Product
Module Replacement Order Tool
Team
1 designer (me), 1 PM, and 2 engineers
Role
Research, user interviews, product thinking, UX writing, UI design
Outcome
Replacement order time became 70% faster than before

Background

Hangry is a cloud kitchen. One physical outlet running multiple brands, each with its own menu and its own operational identity. By 2024, Hangry had scaled to over 150 outlets across Jakarta.

That complexity shows up in operations day to day. This case study is about one module inside Internal Ops Dashboard: the Replacement Order Tool. When a customer complaint qualifies for a food replacement, the CE team needs to act quickly and accurately. Before this product existed, they were doing it through the Hangry App. That’s where the problem started.

Research & Discovery

The brief came to me in October 2024. The CE team of ten agents was handling food replacement orders every week across all of Hangry’s brands, outlets, and sales channels. Every complaint that qualified for a replacement had to be handled individually.

I talked with Regina, the CE team leader. What surprised me most wasn’t the volume. It was the time. One replacement order done through the Hangry App took up to ten minutes. This was a pain point for Regina’s team.

TIME PER REPLACEMENT ORDER

Around 10 min/order

Using the Hangry App, a consumer product never built for this workflow

REPLACEMENTS PER WEEK

150-200 orders/week

Hangry runs multiple brands across multiple outlets and platforms.

Using the Hangry App for this workflow wasn’t just slow, it was the wrong tool for the job. The more I understood how Hangry’s business actually worked, the clearer that became.

Four structural problems, all rooted in the same thing:

PROBLEM 1

Menu Coverage Gap

The Hangry App only shows menus sold directly to customers. Items exclusive to Grab, Gojek, and Shopee don’t exist there. When a menu wasn’t available, CE reached out to the outlet directly, disrupting kitchen operations in the process.

PROBLEM 2

Sub-brand Gap

Hangry’s sub-brands only exist on third-party platforms. Some sub-brand items appear under the main brand in the Hangry App, but many don’t. Same workaround. Reach out to the outlet directly, same disruption.

PROBLEM 3

Pricing Gap

The Hangry App shows consumer-facing prices. Every replacement placed through it was charged at full retail. No way to use internal pricing or COGS. Every replacement cost more than it should have.

PROBLEM 4

No Centralization

Each CE agent used their own Hangry App account. There was no shared view. No way to track orders, monitor volume, or control costs across the team. Regina had no visibility into what her team was doing.

Strategy & Design

The PM’s brief came with a proposed direction: a form that CE fills to place a replacement order, with part of it shared with the customer to input their delivery address and location. A stakeholder also suggested extending this into a WhatsApp chatbot.

I mapped out both flows before we sat down together (me, the PM, and the engineers). Not to commit to either direction, but to have something concrete to pressure-test.

Then we ran them against four constraints we had: did it solve the CE problem? Did it reduce our replacement order cost? Was it low effort for our customers? Was it low effort for our engineers?

Idea CE problem solved Cost reduced Low user effort Low tech effort
Option 1 Yes Yes No No
Option 2 Yes Yes No Yes

Both options failed on the same constraint — user effort. In both cases, the customer still had to fill part of the form and pin point their own location.

These are customers who just had a bad experience. Asking them to do anything extra adds friction to a moment where trust is already thin. We almost pushed through anyway, with CE compensating by handling as much as possible, but something stopped me.

I remembered building the location flow for the Hangry App. The pin point feature we used there was powered by a paid mapping service. A fifth constraint had just appeared: it would add another business cost if we continued with this plan.

That’s when the Google Maps link idea came to mind. Share links already contain latitude and longitude data. CE could simply ask the customer to share their Google Maps location. No form to fill, no pin point to drag, no paid service. After we checked it together, it worked and it was free. That removed every burden listed above.

Here’s what that translated to in practice: the replacement order form inside Internal Ops Dashboard that CE uses to place orders, and the scheduled order tab added to the POS so outlets can process them.

Outcome

The module shipped in February 2025. A few weeks later, I checked back in with Regina.

The tool was stable, with almost no issues after we cleared up the early bugs. Regina said the centralization meant she could actually see what was happening across the team, something that simply wasn’t possible before.

But the number that stuck with me was the time.

BEFORE

Around 10 min

Per replacement order via the Hangry App

AFTER

Around 2-3 min

Per replacement order via the Replacement Order module

REPLACEMENT ORDER WORKLOAD

70% faster than before

Based on Regina’s interview

The cost side was harder to quantify precisely, but the structural shift was clear: replacement orders were no longer being placed at customer-facing prices. Internal pricing through Internal Ops Dashboard meant every replacement cost less than it would have through the Hangry App.

Learnings

“Good solutions don’t always come from thinking harder. Sometimes they come from remembering further.”

The Google Maps link idea didn’t come from a brainstorm. It came from something I already knew from a previous project. Knowing what the right answer looks like in one context helped me see why it was the wrong answer in another.

“Sometimes the most important thing you can know about a user isn’t what they need. It’s how they feel when they need it.”

The other shift was about emotional context. Asking a disappointed customer to do extra jobs isn’t just inconvenient. It adds friction to a moment where trust is already thin. That’s not a UX problem, it’s a people problem, and it changes what you build.
