Hangry App: Designing for a Business Model Nobody Had Seen Before

Context

Hangry is a cloud kitchen one physical outlet, multiple brands. From a single location, users can order from all available brands at once. This is fundamentally different from conventional food apps where one outlet equals one brand, one type of menu.

When I joined in 2019, Hangry had only 3 outlets and 3 brands, all in Jakarta. I was the only full-time UI/UX Designer. An existing design was already in place, but after studying it closely, I found two fundamental problems.

Two Problems, One Root Cause

The first was about identity. The existing design made Hangry feel like one restaurant with a wide menu, the kind of place where you scroll through lots of options from a single kitchen. That's not what Hangry was. Hangry was multiple distinct brands sharing one location. Getting it wrong in the UI meant users would never truly understand what they were using.

The second was about access. Like most food apps in Indonesia at the time, the design used a location-first pattern. In the existing design, if the user was too far from an outlet, they were blocked entirely, couldn't enter the app at all.

For an app with hundreds of partners spread across a city, that makes sense. For Hangry with only 3 outlets, it meant most of Jakarta couldn't get past the front door and had no chance to discover what Hangry's brands actually were.

The Thinking Behind My Decision

  • 01 More steps doesn't mean worse UX. What matters is making sure every step is the right one. Comfort comes from clarity, not from fewer taps.
  • 02 "We might miss a sales opportunity. But we didn't have to miss a brand awareness opportunity." Not being able to order today doesn't mean not being able to order ever.
  • 03 Design needs to account for where the product is now and where it's going. My decisions had to work today and be adjustable as coverage grew.

The Decision

I proposed a flow different from the existing design. Instead of blocking users based on distance, the app would still read their location, then show a list of available outlets.

If every outlet was out of range: users could still enter, browse all brands, and see full menus. They just couldn't check out and that was communicated clearly from the start.

This served two purposes: it removed the hard wall that was shutting out most of Jakarta, and it let the brands speak for themselves, which was exactly what Hangry needed in its early phase.

User flow — location check and how the app responds based on distance

The Pushback

When I presented this, some people found it unfamiliar. It didn't match what they'd seen elsewhere.

I walked through the reasoning, then added one key point: "This behavior doesn't have to be permanent. Once Hangry's coverage is wide enough, we can adjust." This wasn't a final decision, it was the right decision for this phase. It landed, and became the direction for first launch.

Outcome

The launch went well. Early Google Play reviews came in consistently: easy to use, smooth, clear choices, seamless from order to pick-up.

"Easy to use, the brands are clearly laid out."
"Smooth experience from ordering to pick-up, no confusion."
"Clean and simple, I immediately understood what Hangry was."

Early Google Play reviews post-launch

For a product introducing behavior different from everything else in the market, that kind of feedback means one thing: users weren't confused by something new. That was the goal.

Within a year of launch, Hangry grew from 3 to 40+ outlets and raised $13M in Series A, growing faster than almost any F&B startup in Indonesia at the time. A product that clearly communicates what it is, and doesn't block the people it's trying to reach, is part of how you get there.

What This Taught Me

"Given where this product is right now, what's the most honest and useful experience I can design?"

Before this project, a product limitation felt like a design problem to solve around. After it, I started seeing constraints differently, not as walls, but as context.

Products in the same industry don't share the same user journey. Everything depends on how the business works and what constraints it has at that specific phase.
